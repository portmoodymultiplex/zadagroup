Port Moody multiplex rules, explained for homeowners.

By the Zada Group team · 7 minute read

Port Moody multiplex rules changed the math on almost every single-family lot in the city. If you own a home here, the zoning that used to allow one house now allows several, and most owners still have not sat down to figure out what that means for their own property. This guide is the plain-language version.

What the framework allows.

Under BC's Small-Scale Multi-Unit Housing legislation, Port Moody permits multiple homes on lots that were previously single-family only. On most standard lots that means three to four units. Near frequent transit, provincial rules push the ceiling to six. The exact number for your property depends on lot size, servicing, and where you sit relative to transit, which is why the first real step is always a lot-specific review rather than a rule of thumb.

Why Port Moody is a special case.

Two reasons. First, geography: Port Moody's inventory of flat, well-serviced single-family lots is small and hemmed in by the inlet and the mountains, so buildable land here carries a scarcity premium that most Metro Vancouver suburbs do not have. Second, momentum: SkyTrain arrived in 2016, Moody Centre is planned for significant growth, and the city's own long-range planning anticipates far more homes than the current housing stock provides. Multiplex zoning is how many of those homes will actually get built.

The process, start to finish.

The single biggest change SSMUH made is procedural. Building a multiplex on an eligible lot no longer requires a rezoning, which used to mean public hearings and years of uncertainty. Instead, the path runs through a building permit: confirm what your lot allows, get a survey and a servicing assessment, engage an architect or designer who has done missing-middle work, and submit. It is still a real process with real costs, and servicing requirements like water flow and driveway access can create friction, but it is a permit process, not a political one.

What trips people up.

Three things, over and over. Servicing: older streets sometimes need water or sewer upgrades, and flow testing can produce surprises. Site constraints: slopes, trees, and easements all shape what fits. And sequencing: owners who commit to an architect before they understand the financial picture often pay for drawings of something that never pencils. Run the feasibility first, then design.

What it means if you are not building.

Even if you never lift a shovel, the framework changed what your lot is worth and who will want it. Builders and small developers now compete for well-located Port Moody lots, and a home that once sold only to families now also sells on its redevelopment potential. If you are selling, that potential should be part of the pricing conversation, not an afterthought.

Dawar Zada was the first homeowner in Port Moody to move a multiplex application forward under the new framework, on his own property, and we run portmoodymultiplex.ca as a dedicated resource for the city's rules. Start there, or start with a conversation.

Frequently asked questions.

How many units can I build on my Port Moody lot?

Most standard single-family lots in Port Moody allow three to four units under SSMUH, and lots near frequent transit can allow up to six. The exact count depends on your lot size, servicing, and location, so a lot-specific review is the reliable answer.

Do I need a rezoning to build a multiplex in Port Moody?

No. On eligible lots, small-scale multi-unit housing is a permitted use, which means the path runs through a building permit rather than a rezoning with public hearings.

Is my lot eligible?

Most single-family lots in Port Moody are covered by the framework, but site specifics like servicing, slope, and easements shape what actually fits. We review eligibility as the first step of any feasibility conversation.
