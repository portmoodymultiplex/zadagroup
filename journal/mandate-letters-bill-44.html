B.C. moves to hand zoning back to cities. Bill 44 is still the law.

SZ
Written by: Sheri Zada | Reviewed by Dawar Zada, PREC*
Not a licensed REALTOR® | Communications and Community Relations
Published Nov 26, 20266 minute read
The numbers

On November 24, Premier Lorne Doerkson issued mandate letters to his new cabinet. Every minister now works toward five goals: an affordable B.C., a prosperous B.C., a healthy B.C., a safer B.C., and a strong and proud B.C. Each one has to commit to a measurable result and a delivery date, and any new spending has to get through Treasury Board first.

For anyone who owns, builds or buys in Metro Vancouver, the letters that matter most went to the two ministers who now share housing. Housing no longer has a ministry of its own. It sits inside the new Ministry of Local Government, Housing, Sport and Culture under Cathie Ounsted, with Zeeshan Wahla as Minister of State for Housing.

The first item on Ounsted's list is to restore municipal authority over the location, form and character of housing, respecting each city's Official Community Plan. That is the clearest signal yet that the province intends to hand zoning decisions back to city councils.

What the letters do not do is change any law. Bill 44, the 2023 law that allows up to four homes on most single-family lots, is still in force. Below: what the housing letters say, what they leave out, where the Tri-Cities stand, and what it means if you own, build or buy here.

What the housing letters say

Two ministers, one direction. Both letters point toward more local control, faster approvals and lower construction costs.

Cathie Ounsted

Minister of Local Government, Housing, Sport and Culture

  • Zoning goes local. Restore municipal authority over the location, form and character of housing, respecting local Official Community Plans and community priorities.
  • Faster approvals. Shorten development approvals, cut unnecessary municipal requirements, and match housing growth to infrastructure capacity.
  • Building code. Overhaul the Step Code, B.C.'s energy-efficiency standard for new construction, and stop cities from adding requirements beyond the B.C. Building Code without provincial approval.
  • Costs. Scrap unnecessary charges that add to construction costs.
  • Gas and heating. Work with local governments to review municipal bans on gas connections and mandatory heating technologies.
  • Metro Vancouver. Refocus the regional district on its core role as a regional utility, and commission an independent audit of the North Shore Wastewater Treatment Plant.

Zeeshan Wahla

Minister of State for Housing

  • Approvals. Shorten housing development approvals "from years to months."
  • Zoning and plans. Align zoning with Official Community Plans.
  • Step Code. Work on the same Step Code overhaul as Ounsted.
  • Background. Wahla is a professional engineer.

Mandate letter. A premier's written instructions to a minister. It sets priorities, not law. Anything that changes a statute, like Bill 44, still has to pass the legislature.

What the letters leave out

The gaps matter as much as the bullet points, especially if you have a project in progress.

No law is named

Neither housing letter mentions Bill 44 (small-scale, multi-unit housing), Bill 43 (the Housing Supply Act and its municipal targets) or Bill 47 (transit-oriented areas). Neither orders a repeal, sets a timeline, or lays out how the province will consult before changing the legislation.

That is a softer position than the campaign. Doerkson campaigned on repealing Bill 44, and has since said his government will "look at repealing it." He has also committed to removing the municipal housing targets set under Bill 43.

The legislature does not sit again until spring 2027. Until then, the NDP-era housing laws stay in force.

No word on the speculation tax

Finance Minister Peter Milobar's letter does not mention the speculation and vacancy tax. It does tell him to raise the personal income tax exemption, appoint a panel to review B.C.'s tax system and report within six months, and reverse last year's changes to property tax deferral "so seniors are not taxed out of their homes."

Nothing in the letters addresses rental housing or tenancy rules directly.

Bill 44 in one line. In municipalities over 5,000 people, lots once zoned for a single house or duplex must allow up to four homes, and up to six near frequent transit.

Where the Tri-Cities stand

Some councils did not wait for the province.

Port Coquitlam

On November 24, the same day the letters went out, Port Coquitlam council unanimously supported Mayor Brad West's motion to suspend new applications that rely on Bill 44's small-scale densities until the province clarifies its changes. Staff were also told to review the city's small-scale housing zoning.

The motion does not stop all development and does not revoke permits already approved. It applies to new applications using the Bill 44 densities.

Port Moody and Coquitlam

Neither council had taken formal action as of November 25. Both cities' mayors at the time signed the December 2025 open letter from Metro Vancouver mayors asking the province to rethink its housing laws.

Elsewhere in the region, Delta has deferred a lot subdivision until as late as April 2027 and will debate an Official Community Plan review on December 7. Surrey has moved to protect single-family zoning in Crescent Beach and Ocean Park. Burnaby has not suspended anything, partly because its federal Housing Accelerator Fund money requires at least four units per lot.

The building cost side of the package

The Step Code. The industry has pushed for this one. The Urban Development Institute estimated that meeting the highest efficiency and emissions tiers could add up to $50,000 per apartment in a concrete rental tower, and about $65,000 once financing and other costs are included. Engineers on the other side warn that building to the minimum code can mean higher utility bills for years and homes that handle extreme weather worse.

Gas connections. Reviewing municipal gas bans could put the province at odds with Vancouver, where Mayor Pete Fry's new council has introduced a motion to bring back the city's natural gas ban for new buildings.

Metro Vancouver. The regional district collects development cost charges on new homes for water, sewer and parkland, and those charges have climbed sharply in recent years. The letter's push to shrink it back to a utility, and to audit a treatment plant whose budget grew from $700 million to $3.9 billion, is aimed at the costs that eventually land on new homes and on utility bills.

What it means for owners, builders and buyers

If you own a lot

Nothing has changed legally. Your zoning is whatever your city's bylaw says today, and in Port Moody and Coquitlam that still includes small-scale, multi-unit housing.

What has changed is the direction. If your plan for the property depends on building three or four units, the window to apply under the current rules may be narrower than it looked a month ago, and in Port Coquitlam it is already closed for new applications.

If you have an application in

Port Coquitlam's motion does not touch permits already approved. Elsewhere, nothing in the letters reaches back to applications already in the system. Keep your file moving, respond to city comments quickly, and do not let a deadline lapse while the province decides.

If you are buying

Be careful paying for multiplex potential as if it were guaranteed. A lot priced on what it could become under Bill 44 carries more risk now than it did in October. Price it on what you can confirm with the city today.

Where this is likely headed

Repealing Bill 44 outright was a campaign line. Governing has turned it into "restore municipal authority," and that gap is worth noticing. The more likely outcome is a province that lets each council decide how much small-scale housing it wants, rather than one that makes every city go back to single-family zoning.

That would mean the Tri-Cities stop moving together. Port Coquitlam has already shown its hand. Port Moody and Coquitlam will set their own course, and the answer could differ street by street depending on each city's Official Community Plan.
