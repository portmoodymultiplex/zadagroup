Bank of Canada raises rates as Metro Vancouver home prices fall.

SZ
Written by: Sheri Zada
Not a licensed REALTOR® | Communications and Community Relations
Reviewed by Dawar Zada, PREC*
Published Nov 16, 20268 minute read
The numbers

On October 28, the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate by a quarter point, to 2.5%. It was the first increase since July 2023, and it landed in the middle of a Metro Vancouver market that was already soft. Two weeks later, the October numbers came in: fewer sales, lower prices, and a lot of buyers and sellers sitting on their hands.

The hike had nothing to do with housing. The Bank raised rates because oil has been expensive for most of the year and it doesn't want fuel costs spreading into everything else. Inflation is at 3.1%, and the Bank now expects it to reach about 3.5% by the end of the year before working back to 2% by mid-2028. Housing is collateral damage.

The hike came the same month Metro Vancouver's benchmark price slipped another 0.7%, to $1,068,600, down 5.2% from a year ago. So borrowing got more expensive while the homes got cheaper, and those two things don't hit everyone the same way.

Below: what the hike actually changed, which mortgages it touches and which it doesn't, what to expect on December 9, and what it means if you are renewing, buying, or investing.

If you read nothing else, read this

One

If you're variable, you felt it already. Prime went up the next day. On a $500,000 variable mortgage with 25 years left, that's roughly $70 a month more.

Two

If you're fixed, the hike didn't touch you. Fixed rates follow the bond market, not the Bank, and the bond market had already priced most of this in. What matters for you is the rate on the day you renew.

Three

If you're buying, the price drop is bigger than the rate hike. A year of falling prices has saved a typical Port Moody condo buyer about twice what a quarter point costs them. Run the numbers below.

Two kinds of mortgage, two different stories

"Rates went up" is technically true and mostly unhelpful. Which rate, and whose mortgage, changes the answer completely.

Variable · HELOCs

Moves with the Bank

  • What changed. Lenders moved prime from 4.45% to 4.70%, effective October 29. Every variable mortgage and home equity line of credit tied to prime went up by the same quarter point.
  • What it costs. On $500,000 with 25 years left, the payment goes from about $2,765 to about $2,836 a month. Each further quarter point adds roughly another $70.
  • Fixed payment variable. If your payment didn't change, your amortization did. More of each payment now goes to interest, and less to the balance.
  • What could come next. The Bank said it "could" raise again but will "proceed carefully." RBC expects three more hikes starting as soon as December. Scotiabank expects one in December. CIBC and TD expect a pause first and one more early in 2027.

Our read

One quarter point is annoying, not dangerous. Three would be a real number, about $230 a month on that same $500,000. If you're variable and your budget is tight, now is the time to price out a fixed rate, not after December 9.

Fixed · Renewals

Moves with the bond market

  • Where fixed rates come from. Five year fixed rates are priced off the five year Government of Canada bond yield, plus a lender margin. That yield is sitting around 3.5%, after touching about 3.73% in late September.
  • Why the hike barely moved them. Bond investors saw the hike coming and had already pushed yields up. Fixed rates started climbing before the Bank did anything.
  • Where they are now. As of November 13, the best insured five year fixed rates were around 4.44%, and the average five year fixed was about 5.17%. Your number depends on your down payment, your lender, and how hard you shop.
  • The stress test. To qualify, you still have to prove you could carry the greater of your rate plus 2 points, or 5.25%. Every bump in rates shrinks what a buyer can borrow.

Our read

If you locked in at 2% in 2021, the hike isn't your problem. Your renewal is. The jump from a pandemic-era rate to today's is many times bigger than anything the Bank did in October.

On a typical Port Moody condo, the price drop over the past year has already saved a buyer about twice what one quarter point costs them. And that's before you negotiate. In a market where nearly half of Port Moody sales went to a second listing attempt, the benchmark is where the conversation starts, not where it ends.

The plain version. Rates going up hurts people who already have a mortgage and need a new one. Prices going down helps people who don't have one yet. Right now, the second effect is bigger.

The renewal wall is the real story

The Bank of Canada has spent two years warning about the borrowers who locked in near the bottom. The last of them are coming up now.

The national picture

In its Financial Stability Report this spring, the Bank estimated that about 12% of all outstanding Canadian mortgages would renew over the following year, most of them the last of the pandemic-era five year fixed loans, with payments rising about 15% on average. That was before October's hike.

The same report estimated that about 4% of borrowers renewing in 2027 wouldn't be able to refinance at current home prices, and about 7% if prices fall another 10%. That's a real number, but read it carefully. TD's economists pointed out that it measures people who couldn't refinance, meaning take out a new loan or pull equity, not people who can't renew with their existing lender. Most people renew without being requalified.

The pressure should start to ease next year. CIBC expects renewal payment increases to ease notably from February 2027, once the cheapest 2021 and early 2022 mortgages have rolled over.

What actually helps

Starting early. Many lenders will hold a rate for 120 days before renewal. If you're renewing in the first half of 2027, you can shop now and protect yourself if rates keep climbing.

Stretching the amortization. Bank of Canada research found that about half of borrowers facing a payment increase could almost wipe it out by extending their amortization by five years. You pay more interest overall, but you keep your monthly budget intact.

Not signing the first letter. Your lender's renewal offer is an opening bid. Compare it with at least one other lender or a broker. Switching at renewal is usually much simpler than people expect.

Renew versus refinance. Renewing keeps the same loan with a new rate and term. Refinancing replaces it with a new loan, usually to change the amount, and means qualifying all over again.

What we'd do right now

Same rate hike, three very different situations.

If you own and you're renewing

Find your renewal date today, and work out your new payment at 4.5%, 5% and 5.5%. If the highest one breaks your budget, you have options, but they're better the earlier you use them: a rate hold, a longer amortization, a lump sum before renewal, or a different lender.

If the honest answer is that the house doesn't work at the new payment, that's worth knowing now rather than in the spring. Selling on your own timeline in a slow market is a very different experience from selling on your lender's.

If you're buying

This is the part nobody puts in the headline: buyers have the most leverage they've had in years. Inventory in Metro Vancouver is about 24% above normal, sales are well below average, and sellers who missed the first time around are realistic now.

Get pre-approved before December 9, because a pre-approval usually holds your rate for 90 to 120 days. If rates go up again, you're protected. If they don't, you've lost nothing. Then negotiate like it's a buyer's market, because in most of the region, it is.

If you invest

Higher rates make the cash flow math on a rental harder, especially on a variable mortgage or a HELOC used for the down payment. Stress test your holdings at prime plus another half point and make sure you'd still sleep at night.

On the buy side, this is the kind of market where good deals actually exist. Sellers facing renewals, developers holding finished inventory, and fewer buyers to compete with. Buy on today's rents and today's rates, not on the hope of a cut that the Bank has just told you isn't coming soon.

Our honest read

The Bank of Canada raised rates because of oil, not because of housing. Its own senior deputy governor called the housing outlook "pretty subdued." This is a central bank trying to stop an energy shock from becoming permanent inflation, and Metro Vancouver real estate is caught in the crossfire.

October's slowdown wasn't only about rates, either. Greater Vancouver REALTORS® chief economist Andrew Lis pointed to the hike and a snap provincial election, plus the uncertainty that came with both. We saw the same thing on the ground. A lot of people decided to wait and see.

Waiting has a cost, though, and it isn't the same for everyone. If you're renewing, every week you wait is a week you're not protected. If you're buying, the people waiting are your competition, and right now there are fewer of them than there have been in years. The BC Real Estate Association expects a modest recovery in 2027. The best deals in a recovery happen before everyone agrees it's started.

We'll be watching December 9. If the Bank moves again, we'll write about it here. In the meantime, if you want to know what your renewal or your next purchase looks like at today's rates, ask one of our agents.
