B.C.'s new government hands housing decisions back to cities.

SZ
Written by: Sheri Zada | Reviewed by Dawar Zada, PREC*
Not a licensed REALTOR® | Communications and Community Relations
Published Nov 26, 20269 minute read
The numbers

On November 24, Premier Lorne Doerkson issued mandate letters to every cabinet minister and minister of state. These letters are the new government's to-do list: what each minister is expected to deliver, how they'll be measured, and what they're not allowed to spend money on without a fight.

The headline for anyone who owns, buys or builds in Metro Vancouver is in the housing letters. The Province is telling its housing ministers to restore municipal control over the "location, form and character of housing." In plain terms, the era of Victoria setting the rules for what gets built on your street is ending, and the decisions are going back to city hall.

Below: what the letters say about housing, what they leave out, the tax changes most families will feel first, and what it means if you own, buy or invest.

If you read nothing else, read this

One

Zoning power is moving back to cities. The letters don't name Bill 44 or SSMUH, but the direction to restore local control over the form and character of housing points one way. What your lot allows will depend more on your council than on Victoria.

Two

Approvals and building costs are the target. Ministers are told to cut approvals from years to months, overhaul the Step Code, stop cities adding requirements beyond the B.C. Building Code, and scrap charges that push up construction costs.

Three

None of the big real estate taxes were touched. The property transfer tax, the speculation and vacancy tax, the home flipping tax and the 20% foreign buyer tax aren't mentioned in any letter we read. For now, the rules you buy and sell under haven't changed.

What the letters actually say about housing

Housing sits with two people. Cathie Ounsted is Minister of Local Government, Housing, Sport and Culture. Zeeshan Wahla is Minister of State for Housing. Their letters overlap, and together they set out the plan.

Local control

Ounsted's letter directs her to restore municipal control over the location, form and character of housing, respecting each city's Official Community Plan. Wahla's letter asks him to align municipal zoning with those OCPs. That matches what the Premier said before he was sworn in, when he told reporters he planned to scrap provincial housing targets and roll back the rules that let multiplexes go up without a rezoning.

Faster approvals

Wahla is told to shorten development approvals "from years to months." Ounsted is told to cut unnecessary municipal requirements and match housing growth to infrastructure capacity. That last part matters. Expect water, sewer and road capacity to become a bigger part of the conversation when a project goes in.

Building costs

Both letters call for an overhaul of the Step Code, the energy efficiency standard that has added real cost to new construction. Ounsted is also told to stop municipalities from adding building requirements beyond the B.C. Building Code without provincial approval, to scrap charges that raise construction costs, and to review municipal bans on gas connections and mandated heating systems.

Metro Vancouver

Ounsted's letter also takes aim at the regional district. It directs her to refocus Metro Vancouver on its core job as a regional utility, hand its other functions back to cities, review its major capital commitments, and commission an independent audit of the North Shore Wastewater Treatment Plant. Regional utility costs show up on every property tax bill in the region, so this one is worth watching.

The plain version. Fewer provincial rules on what you can build, more say for your city, and pressure to make building cheaper and faster.

What the letters leave out

What isn't in these letters tells you as much as what is.

Housing

No numbers, no named repeal

  • No housing targets. Neither housing letter sets a deadline or a numeric target for new homes. The only hard number in Wahla's letter is restarting seven deferred long-term care homes for seniors.
  • No named repeal. Bill 44, SSMUH and transit-oriented areas aren't named. The direction is clear, but the details of what gets changed, and when, haven't been written yet.
  • No rental policy. Neither housing letter addresses rentals or the short-term rental rules.

Finance

No change to real estate taxes

  • Untouched. The Finance letter doesn't mention the property transfer tax, the speculation and vacancy tax, the home flipping tax or the foreign buyer tax.
  • Not yet. A Blue-Ribbon Panel is due to report within 180 days on simplifying the tax system. That's the next place real estate taxes could come up.

Our read

Every minister is told new spending has to pass Treasury Board, with affordability for taxpayers as the test. Read that as a government that will be slow to announce new housing programs.

We moved early. Our clients did too.

When Bill 44 passed in late 2023, most people in Port Moody read it as a headline. We read it as a deadline.

How we got there first

Dawar was one of the first homeowners in Port Moody to move a multiplex application forward under the new rules, and Zada Group has been involved in almost every small-scale multi-unit application in the city since.

Going first meant learning the process while the city was still writing it. Setbacks, servicing reviews, access and driveway questions, water flow tests, variances. Every one of those lessons went straight to the clients who came after us, so they didn't have to learn them the expensive way.

We train our agents on legislation and bylaws city by city, and we read council agendas the way most people read the news. That's how a small group of our consulting clients had applications in while the rules were still wide open.

Why it matters now

Most owners never moved. Port Moody staff told council in November 2025 that units at the rezoning stage had dropped 70 percent from the year before, part of a slowdown across the region.

Now the door is closing. On November 24, Port Coquitlam council voted unanimously to suspend new applications that rely on Bill 44's small-scale densities. Port Moody's new mayor, Haven Lurbiecki, was the only councillor last term to vote against the city's Official Community Plan. Port Moody hasn't acted yet, and Bill 44 is still the law there, but the direction is hard to miss.

The plain version. If the rules tighten, the multiplex homes already approved or in the system could be some of the only ones Port Moody gets. Most of them have our fingerprints on them.

A refundable Children's Activity Credit is also on the list, and the seniors' property tax deferral terms are being restored.

For homeowners and small business

Multigenerational homes. A new Multigenerational Home Renovation Tax Credit is coming. If you've been thinking about a suite for a parent or adult kids, this is one to watch.

Professional services. The proposed PST expansion to professional services is cancelled for good.

Business equipment. A three-year PST exemption for qualifying machinery, equipment and software.

Basic personal amount. The income you can earn before you pay any provincial income tax. Raising it is a tax cut for everyone who files.

Getting around the Lower Mainland

Trevor Halford's Transportation letter has a few items that touch Metro Vancouver directly.

  • No car tax. TransLink will be barred from charging a tax on owning or registering a car.
  • A review first. TransLink's capital costs, executive pay and operating costs get an independent review before any new funding requests.
  • A tougher test for new lines. Transit expansion is supported only where population and ridership show the need.
  • Massey Tunnel. Cabinet will get what it needs to make a final call on the George Massey Tunnel replacement.

There's nothing specific to the Tri-Cities. For buyers who pick a home based on future transit, the takeaway is that new lines will face a tougher test than they did before.

What we'd do right now

Same letters, three very different situations.

If you own

Nothing in these letters changes your property taxes, your sale or your purchase today. If you've been weighing a suite or a renovation to bring family under one roof, hold the receipts and wait for the details on the multigenerational credit before you commit.

If you're buying

Your costs haven't changed. Same transfer tax, same exemptions, same rules. What may change over the next year or two is what can be built next door. If that matters to you, ask about your city's OCP, not just the current zoning.

If you invest or hold land

This is the group that should pay attention. If you've been banking on multiplex potential under provincial rules, that value now depends on your city's next move. If you already have an application in, you're in one of the best positions in the city. Keep it moving and don't let a deadline lapse.

If you're buying a lot for its density, price it on what the OCP supports, not what the provincial rules allowed.

Paying attention pays.

We didn't get into SSMUH early by luck. We got in early because we watch Victoria and city hall as closely as we watch the market, and we act on what we see. Our clients who listened are holding approvals that may be hard to get again. A lot of owners who planned to look into it someday are about to find out someday has passed.

We said in October that we expected this government to give cities more power rather than simply tear the rules up, and these letters read that way. The language is about restoring local control and lining zoning up with each city's plan, which means the answer for your lot will depend on which city you're in.

For most of our clients, this doesn't change the plan. People moving for a growing family, a parent, a school or a commute are making the same move they were last week. The one thing it does change is the case for paying a premium for density on its own. That's a conversation worth having before you buy, not after.

If you own a lot in Port Moody or Coquitlam and you've been meaning to find out what it allows, find out now, while Bill 44 is still the law there. Once a council acts, that conversation is over. Ask one of our agents, and we'll tell you where your lot stands and what we'd do with it.
